Welcome back to the third and final post in our series on what it takes for a cord to be UL Listed: putting it all together. The plug is covered by UL 498, the jacket by UL 62, the IEC connector by UL 60320-1, and the finished product by UL 817. But how do we know the finished product is actually safe? Testing.

<a href="https://blog.stayonline.com/2026/08/31/The-Makings-of-a-UL-Listed-Power-Cord.html">As we've covered</a>, UL 817 is more than just the sum of its <a href="https://blog.stayonline.com/2026/09/15/The-Anatomy-of-a-UL-Power-Cord.html">UL-listed parts</a>. For a hand-terminated assembly, this means that a cord made in the field is not UL Listed, **even if it is mechanically identical** to a cord set produced in a UL facility. For a cord to be UL Listed, it must be put to the test: literally connected to a hipot tester and checked for dielectric voltage withstand, continuity, and polarity. The company producing the cord must also be audited by UL to confirm that it is living up to the standard and performing the tests properly.

## Dielectric Voltage Withstand (Hipot Testing)

Dielectric voltage withstand testing, commonly called "hipot" (short for high potential) testing, checks whether a product's insulation can withstand a voltage well above its operating level without breaking down. Basically, you apply far more voltage than the cord will ever see in daily use, and if the insulation doesn't break down or arc under that stress, it isn't going to under normal conditions.

## Continuity Testing

As the name implies, continuity testing confirms that a conductive path exists where it's supposed to, i.e., that there are no broken wires inside the cord. A small current is applied between the two ends (plug and connector) to verify that it flows and that the resistance stays within an acceptable range.

## Polarity Testing

In addition to hipot and continuity testing, every UL cord is also tested for polarity. Polarity testing ensures that line goes to line and neutral goes to neutral. If those wires were inadvertently crossed during manufacturing, polarity testing will catch the problem before the unit ships. This test exists for good reason: crossed wires have real safety implications.

## Pull Testing

Cords must pass physical tests as well as electrical ones. All new terminal connections are pull tested in line with manufacturer guidelines and industry standards, and subsequent terminations are pull tested at a sample rate of 1 in 100 units. Depending on the termination type, between 20 and 50 pounds of pull force is applied.

## UL Audits

Beyond performing the tests, manufacturers are inspected by UL auditors through UL's <a href="https://www.ul.com/resources/follow-up-services">Follow-Up Services</a> program to verify that testing procedures are being followed. Inspections occur four times per year and may be announced or unannounced.

## Conclusion

A UL Listed cord isn't just a collection of listed components. It's the result of a controlled process: the right parts, assembled to the standard, tested one by one for insulation strength, continuity, and polarity, verified through pull testing, and backed by ongoing UL inspections.  When safety and compliance matter, the label on the cord is only as meaningful as the process behind it.
